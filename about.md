  <div class="container narrow">
    <span class="eyebrow">About</span>
    <h1 style="font-size:clamp(2.4rem,6vw,4rem)">We debug democracy.</h1>
    <p class="lede">DESH stands for Democratically Elected System Hackers. Right now it is a lab: open to anyone who clears the core reading, working in the open, arguing about mechanisms. A party is where it is headed, and the lab is what has to exist first. We treat governing as a system to be engineered rather than a stage to perform on. India is the first test bed, and nothing in the design depends on India.</p>

<section class="section" style="padding-top:1rem">
  <div class="container">
    <div class="grid grid-2">
      <div class="card reveal">
        <span class="kicker">Mission</span>
        <h3>Experts on the controls</h3>
        <p>Put the people who run a system in charge of the rules for that system. A career in politics teaches you how to win office, which is a real skill and an unrelated one. The person deciding how aircraft get certified has usually never certified anything, and the cost of that learning curve is carried by everybody else.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card reveal">
        <span class="kicker">Approach</span>
        <h3>Engineering discipline</h3>
        <p>Software teams did not get reliable by hiring better people. They got there on unglamorous habits: write the change down, have someone who knows the area read it, try it somewhere small, keep the ability to undo it. Almost none of that survives contact with a legislature, and there is no good reason for that.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card reveal">
        <span class="kicker">Team</span>
        <h3>100 founders</h3>
        <p>A hundred is the legal threshold to register a party in India. We would rather fill it with people who have spent a decade inside aviation, health, defence, finance or infrastructure than with people who have spent a decade near politics. The first hundred set the bar for everyone who comes after.</p>
      </div>
      <div class="card reveal">
        <span class="kicker">Vision</span>
        <h3>A technocratic democracy</h3>
        <p>Democratic in who decides, technocratic in what counts as an argument. Evidence and measured outcomes over rhetoric and electoral arithmetic. Not left, not right. Forward. <a class="link-arrow" href="/vision/" style="display:inline-flex">See the vision <span class="arrow">→</span></a></p>

<section class="thesis">
  <div class="container">
    <span class="eyebrow reveal">Why "software-defined"?</span>
    <p class="thesis-lead reveal">Politics runs on legacy code. <span class="muted">It works, roughly. Nobody still around knows why it works. And there is no way to roll back a bad release.</span></p>
    <div class="beats">
      <div class="beat reveal"><span class="n">→</span><div><h3>Open by default, not on request</h3><p>The RTI Act made opacity expensive by putting a statutory clock on it, and that has been worth more than any speech about honesty. The next version shouldn't wait for the application. Decisions, votes and money get published as they happen, because a record you have to ask for is a record someone can lose.</p></div></div>
      <div class="beat reveal"><span class="n">→</span><div><h3>Small releases, fast reverts</h3><p>A policy that reaches everyone on day one cannot be wrong safely. Run it in a few districts, measure the thing you said you would measure, keep the undo wired up. The goal is not speed. The goal is that being wrong stops being catastrophic.</p></div></div>
      <div class="beat reveal"><span class="n">→</span><div><h3>Anyone can send a patch</h3><p>Open source is a permission structure, not a mood: anyone may propose a change, and people who know the area decide on it in public. Parties are the exact inverse of that. This one works like a repository, with the arguments and the rejections visible too.</p></div></div>
    </div>
  </div>
</section>
